Oven Fresh Bakery: from three spreadsheets to one system

01 · Context

A bakery run on spreadsheets

Oven Fresh Bakery makes bread, rolls, cakes and sandwiches and delivers them to about 45 shops in England, including a hospital. The whole business ran on three spreadsheets: a delivery sheet for each shop's week, a printed invoice, and a monthly ledger with a column marking who had paid.

02 · Challenge

Every figure typed three times

Each week's figures were typed into the sheet, again onto the invoice and again into the ledger, so the books drifted apart. When I audited the files cell by cell I found:

  • A subtotal typed in as a number instead of a formula, which overcharged one customer £56.49 a week for three weeks.
  • Amounts typed as text, like "76.63 R", which Excel's SUM skipped without warning: £547.71 was missing from the monthly totals.
  • The same week invoiced twice, and one shop spelt three different ways.

Nobody could say at a glance who owed money, or how late it was.

03 · Goals

Goals and objectives

  • Enter each shop's week once, and have the invoice, the ledger and the revenue figures follow from it.
  • Make the errors found in the spreadsheets impossible to repeat.
  • Show who owes money and for how long, so payments can be chased.
  • Keep every past record, and make the system easy for staff with no computer background.

04 · My role

How I was involved

I ran the project from start to finish. I worked out what the bakery needed with the owner, audited the spreadsheets, designed the data and the screens, built the system using AI-assisted development (Claude Code), moved the history across, and wrote a training guide for the staff. After the first version I kept adding what the bakery asked for, including sale-or-return stock, monthly billing, salary slips and running costs.

05 · Approach

How I approached it

  1. A
    Audit

    Read the three spreadsheets and 26 per-shop invoice books cell by cell, and listed every error and workaround.

  2. B
    Design out the errors

    One record per shop, one invoice per shop per week, every total calculated in one place, and prices frozen onto each invoice.

  3. C
    Migrate

    Wrote a Python import that carried 834 invoices and £91,249 of trading across, with the errors corrected.

  4. D
    Train and support

    Wrote a plain-English staff guide, and set up staff, admin and read-only guest access, an activity log and one-command backups.

06 · Outcomes

What came of it

History moved

834 invoices

45 shops and £91,249 of trading imported, corrected and searchable.

Recovered

£547.71

Revenue the spreadsheets had left out of the monthly totals.

Overbilling caught

£169

Charged to one customer over three weeks by a typed-in subtotal.

07 · What I learned

Looking back

The most useful work happened before any code: every rule in the system came from a mistake I found in the old spreadsheets.

If I did it again, I would spend more time with the staff on day one, watching how they actually entered a week, instead of learning it from the files alone. Several later features, like typing over a monthly sheet before printing, came from habits I only discovered after launch. It taught me that digital transformation is mostly about people and process, and the software is the last step.

01 · Context

A personal product, sold online

AussieStitch is the custom embroidery startup I founded, selling across Australia and New Zealand. Its main product is personal: a customer sends photos of a partner, a pet, a car or the family, and the photo is redrawn by hand, turned into a stitch file and embroidered on a hoodie, tee, cap or tote, or made into an iron-on patch. Logo embroidery for businesses and clubs comes second.

02 · Challenge

Made to order, across three countries

A made-to-order product is hard to sell online. The shop has to collect photos, get a design drawn and approved before anything is stitched, take payment and keep the customer updated, while the designers work in India and the machine runs in Australia.

It also has to earn trust. My first version used drawn graphics, and when I looked at it the way a customer would, it looked AI-generated. I scrapped it and started again with real photography, a normal shop layout and research into competitors.

03 · Goals

Goals and objectives

  • Let customers see their own logo or design on real products before they buy.
  • Run every order end to end: photos, hand-drawn design, proof approval, payment, stitching and delivery.
  • Let the owner change products, prices and wording without a developer.
  • Protect customers' photos and personal details as they move between countries.

04 · My role

How I was involved

I founded the business and built it end to end, using my IT and business management skills together. I researched five Australian competitors, set the customers, pricing and service promises in a written business brief, and designed, built and deployed the platform using AI-assisted development (Claude Code).

05 · Approach

How I approached it

  1. A
    Research

    Compared five competitors. Every one made visitors leave the homepage or fill in a quote form to see their logo on a product, and none showed bulk prices openly.

  2. B
    Stand out

    Made the homepage a working try-on that stitches the visitor's logo onto real product photos, re-plans the page for who is shopping, and shows prices in the open.

  3. C
    Run the operation

    Built the admin panel I run the business from, a portal for the designers with pay per design, proof approval by private link, WhatsApp notifications, and payment by PayID or bank transfer.

  4. D
    Refocus

    Moved the shop to gift buyers first, with hand-drawn portraits as the lead product, and removed an AI image preview once I decided every design would be drawn by a person.

07 · What I learned

Looking back

Throwing away my first version because it looked AI-generated was hard, but it sent me back to research, and the shop that came out of it was far better.

Running my own startup taught me that requirements change as a business learns about its customers. The shop moved from logo orders to gift buyers first, and from card payments to bank transfer for Christmas. Writing my decisions down in a business brief, and testing every change on a separate version before it goes live, kept the business moving quickly without losing track of why things were built.

04Reflective learning

Reflective journal

01Cognitive style

I read before I build

Whetten and Cameron's idea of cognitive style explained why I audited a bakery's spreadsheets cell by cell before writing any code, and when that habit helps or hurts.

November 20262 min read

What I learned

In Chapter 1 of Developing Management Skills, Whetten and Cameron describe cognitive style as the way a person gathers and evaluates information. They separate three approaches: knowing, which relies on facts, detail and logic; planning, which relies on structure and order; and creating, which relies on new ideas and experiments. I recognised myself in the first two straight away. I'm most comfortable when I understand the detail before I decide, and I like to work from a written plan.

Why it was useful

It gave me words for something I had been doing without thinking about it. Before building Oven Fresh Bakery's system, I read its three spreadsheets and 26 invoice books cell by cell. That habit found a typed-in subtotal that had overcharged one customer £169, and £547.71 that had silently dropped out of the monthly totals. The chapter also showed me the cost of the same habit: a knowing style can slow a decision down when the facts will never be complete, and it can make me less open to ideas that don't yet come with evidence.

How I'm applying it

I now decide up front how much detail a decision really needs. For the bakery's later requests, such as billing some shops monthly, I built a small first version and improved it from the staff's feedback instead of studying every case in advance. In group work, I plan to ask classmates with a more creative style for options early, before I start narrowing them down, so the group gets the benefit of both ways of thinking.

02Feedback

Finding my sensitive line

When the bakery's staff asked to type over totals my system calculated, Whetten and Cameron's idea of the sensitive line explained my first reaction, and what to do with it.

November 20262 min read

What I learned

Whetten and Cameron describe the sensitive line as the point at which we become defensive because new information about us clashes with how we see ourselves. Below that line, feedback is easy to take in. Above it, we start protecting our self-image instead of learning. They argue that self-awareness depends on noticing when we have crossed it.

Why it was useful

I met my own sensitive line on the Oven Fresh Bakery project. The rule I was proudest of was that every total is calculated and never typed, because a typed-in subtotal had overcharged a customer for three weeks. After the bakery had been using the system for a while, the staff asked to type over figures on the monthly sheet before printing it. My first reaction was to defend my design: typing over totals was exactly the habit that had caused the errors. Naming that reaction as the sensitive line made it easier to set it aside and ask why they needed it. Real orders change: shops return stock, and a sheet sometimes needs correcting before it goes out. The staff were right that the system had to allow for that.

How I'm applying it

I built the change so both needs were met: any figure can be typed over, but the difference is kept as a correction line, so the invoice still adds up and shows why it changed. Now, when feedback challenges something I care about, I ask what problem the person is trying to solve before I explain my choice. I want to bring the same habit into group work and the teams I lead.

03Values and ethics

Values I won't trade for speed

Building a shop that handles customers' family photos made Whetten and Cameron's section on personal values and ethical decisions very practical.

November 20262 min read

What I learned

Whetten and Cameron link self-awareness to personal values, the standards we use to judge what is right, and to the maturity we bring to ethical decisions. Their argument is that managers who are clear about their values make more consistent decisions under pressure, because they don't have to work out what they stand for each time a hard choice comes up.

Why it was useful

On AussieStitch, customers upload photos of their partners, children and pets. Those photos go to designers in India and are stitched in Australia, so privacy is a real question, not just a policy page. Being clear about my values helped me make these decisions quickly and consistently: only the team sees customers' photos, designers never see customers' names or contact details, photos that aren't ordered are deleted after seven days, and the privacy policy says plainly that photos go overseas, as Australia's privacy principles and New Zealand's Privacy Act require. When I decided every design would be drawn by a person, I removed an AI image preview I had already built.

How I'm applying it

Before I build anything that touches personal data, I ask three questions: who will see it, where does it go, and when is it deleted. I applied the same thinking to the bakery's system, where the bank details sit outside the code and never go into version control. In my career, I want to work for organisations that treat their customers' information with the same care, and to be the person on a team who asks those three questions.

04Change

Changing course with incomplete information

Pivoting my own software product taught me what Whetten and Cameron mean by tolerance of ambiguity, and why a brave decision still needs numbers.

November 20262 min read

What I learned

Whetten and Cameron describe our attitude toward change through two ideas. Tolerance of ambiguity is how well we cope with situations that are new, complex or unclear. Locus of control is how far we believe our own actions shape what happens to us. People with a high tolerance of ambiguity and an internal locus of control tend to adapt better when plans stop working.

Why it was useful

It helped me understand a decision I had made on my own product. I built UncheckDoc as an AI text humanisation and detection tool. Once it was live, I compared what the AI services cost for each user with what users would realistically pay, and the numbers didn't work. Staying put meant waiting on costs I couldn't control. Changing direction meant giving up work I had already done, with no guarantee the new idea would succeed. I chose to act on what I could control and pivoted to an APA 7 referencing and citation fixer, a problem students meet every week. The chapter showed me that what felt like a gut decision rested on two traits I can keep developing.

How I'm applying it

I now check the cost of running a feature before I commit to it, not after. I also treat uncertainty as normal rather than as a reason to wait. On AussieStitch, card payments weren't ready for Christmas, so customers pay by PayID or bank transfer after approving their design. That keeps the shop open while the better option is built. As a manager, I want to make decisions with the best numbers available and set a date to review them.

05Leadership

From doing the work to leading it

Training as a manager at Pizza Hut showed me that emotional intelligence, as Whetten and Cameron describe it, is a skill I have to practise rather than a trait I already have.

November 20262 min read

What I learned

Whetten and Cameron describe emotional intelligence as the ability to recognise and manage our own emotions and to read and respond to other people's. They connect it to core self-evaluation, our underlying confidence in our own worth and ability. Confidence helps a manager act, but self-awareness means knowing where that confidence ends.

Why it was useful

Most of my projects have been solo. I plan them, build them and fix them myself, and I'm confident doing that. Training as a manager at Pizza Hut in Hamilton is different. I supervise team operations and coordinate workflows in a fast-paced environment, and while the orders still have to be right, the harder part is noticing how people are coping and keeping everyone working together. The chapter helped me see that my confidence in my technical ability doesn't automatically carry over to leading people. Reading a team is a separate skill, and I'm at an earlier stage with it.

How I'm applying it

On shifts, I'm practising handing work over instead of stepping in myself, and checking in with people during the shift rather than only at the end. It's also why delegation is my first personal learning goal: to lead a team of four or more as a shift manager by June 2027. The same skill will matter in every management role I want after my Master's, where my results will depend on what a team achieves rather than what I can do alone.
