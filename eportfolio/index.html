Oven Fresh Bakery: from three spreadsheets to one system

I replaced a wholesale bakery's delivery sheets, printed invoices and monthly ledger with a web system for its staff, and found the billing errors the spreadsheets had been hiding.

01 · Context

A bakery run on spreadsheets

Oven Fresh Bakery makes bread, rolls, cakes and sandwiches and delivers them to about 45 shops in England, including a hospital. The whole business ran on three spreadsheets: a delivery sheet for each shop's week, a printed invoice, and a monthly ledger with a column marking who had paid.

02 · Challenge

Every figure typed three times

Each week's figures were typed into the sheet, again onto the invoice and again into the ledger, so the books drifted apart. When I audited the files cell by cell I found:

  • A subtotal typed in as a number instead of a formula, which overcharged one customer £56.49 a week for three weeks.
  • Amounts typed as text, like "76.63 R", which Excel's SUM skipped without warning: £547.71 was missing from the monthly totals.
  • The same week invoiced twice, and one shop spelt three different ways.

Nobody could say at a glance who owed money, or how late it was.

03 · Goals

Goals and objectives

  • Enter each shop's week once, and have the invoice, the ledger and the revenue figures follow from it.
  • Make the errors found in the spreadsheets impossible to repeat.
  • Show who owes money and for how long, so payments can be chased.
  • Keep every past record, and make the system easy for staff with no computer background.

04 · My role

How I was involved

I ran the project from start to finish. I worked out what the bakery needed with the owner, audited the spreadsheets, designed the data and the screens, built the system using AI-assisted development (Claude Code), moved the history across, and wrote a training guide for the staff. After the first version I kept adding what the bakery asked for, including sale-or-return stock, monthly billing, salary slips and running costs.

05 · Approach

How I approached it

  1. A
    Audit

    Read the three spreadsheets and 26 per-shop invoice books cell by cell, and listed every error and workaround.

  2. B
    Design out the errors

    One record per shop, one invoice per shop per week, every total calculated in one place, and prices frozen onto each invoice.

  3. C
    Migrate

    Wrote a Python import that carried 834 invoices and £91,249 of trading across, with the errors corrected.

  4. D
    Train and support

    Wrote a plain-English staff guide, and set up staff, admin and read-only guest access, an activity log and one-command backups.

06 · Outcomes

What came of it

History moved

834 invoices

45 shops and £91,249 of trading imported, corrected and searchable.

Recovered

£547.71

Revenue the spreadsheets had left out of the monthly totals.

Overbilling caught

£169

Charged to one customer over three weeks by a typed-in subtotal.

07 · What I learned

Looking back

The most useful work happened before any code: every rule in the system came from a mistake I found in the old spreadsheets.

01 · Context

A personal product, sold online

AussieStitch is a custom embroidery brand that posts across Australia and New Zealand. Its main product is personal: a customer sends photos of a partner, a pet, a car or the family, and the photo is redrawn by hand, turned into a stitch file and embroidered on a hoodie, tee, cap or tote, or made into an iron-on patch. Logo embroidery for businesses and clubs comes second.

02 · Challenge

Made to order, across three countries

A made-to-order product is hard to sell online. The shop has to collect photos, get a design drawn and approved before anything is stitched, take payment and keep the customer updated, while the designers work in India and the machine runs in Australia.

It also has to earn trust. The owner rejected my first version because its drawn graphics looked AI-generated, and asked for real photography, a normal shop layout and research into competitors.

03 · Goals

Goals and objectives

  • Let customers see their own logo or design on real products before they buy.
  • Run every order end to end: photos, hand-drawn design, proof approval, payment, stitching and delivery.
  • Let the owner change products, prices and wording without a developer.
  • Protect customers' photos and personal details as they move between countries.

04 · My role

How I was involved

I led the product. I researched five Australian competitors, wrote the design brief, recorded the owner's decisions on customers, pricing and service promises in a business brief, and designed, built and deployed the platform using AI-assisted development (Claude Code).

05 · Approach

How I approached it

  1. A
    Research

    Compared five competitors. Every one made visitors leave the homepage or fill in a quote form to see their logo on a product, and none showed bulk prices openly.

  2. B
    Stand out

    Made the homepage a working try-on that stitches the visitor's logo onto real product photos, re-plans the page for who is shopping, and shows prices in the open.

  3. C
    Run the operation

    Built the owner's admin panel, a portal for the designers with pay per design, proof approval by private link, WhatsApp notifications, and payment by PayID or bank transfer.

  4. D
    Refocus

    Moved the shop to gift buyers first, with hand-drawn portraits as the lead product, and removed an AI image preview once the team decided every design would be drawn by a person.

06 · Outcomes

What came of it

Speed

1 week

From first commit to a live shop with an admin panel, orders and proof approval.

Live since

5 Oct 2026

Released automatically, with database changes applied before each release.

Privacy

7 days

Photos not ordered are deleted after a week, and designers never see customer names or contact details.

07 · What I learned

Looking back

Hearing that my first version looked AI-generated was hard, but it sent me back to research, and the shop that came out of it was far better.
